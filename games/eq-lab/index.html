3 · EQ basics

What does Q mean?

Q is the width of the boost or cut. A higher number means a narrower band (technically Q = centre frequency ÷ bandwidth). The table is worked out from the real formula, so it is exact:

QWidthSounds likeTypical use

HEAD 1 uses Q 2.5–2.8, a middle setting: focused but natural. Use a low Q to shape overall tone, a high Q for surgical fixes. (Half an octave is about Q 2.9; a one-third-octave band is about Q 4.3.)

Are LO / LO-MID / HI-MID / HI where the sound is cut off?

No, the band names are not cutoffs. Each band is a hill or valley centred on its F. F says where, G how tall or deep (0 dB = no change), Q how wide. Sound on either side is only partly affected, and that tapering is what Q controls.

The real cutoff is the HPF (112 Hz here): everything below it is removed, everything above passes. It is the steep drop on the left of the green curve. LO and HI can also be shelves, which lift or lower everything below or above a corner. The LO-MID and HI-MID bands on the M-480 can become a notch or band-pass, using the same F and Q.

Peak or shelf? Why are there shelves at all?

A peak is a hill or valley that returns to normal on both sides. A shelf is a step: a LO shelf raises or lowers everything below F, a HI shelf everything above F, and the change then stays level out to the end of the spectrum. A shelf has no Q.

Why do you need shelves?

  • Many problems are broad, not one pitch: bass build-up, a dull sound, too little air. One shelf fixes a whole end. A peak always returns to 0 dB on its far side, so covering "everything above 8 kHz" would take several wide bands and still would not hold the level.
  • A shelf is gentle and musical, not surgical. Use it to re-balance the top and bottom of a voice or mix. Use a peak or notch when you need to hit one spot.
  • It is the classic tone control. The "bass" and "treble" knobs on a hi-fi are shelves (the Baxandall design of 1952), and consoles kept them because they are simple and broad.
  • You only have four bands. A shelf lets one band do the work of several.
  • Mind the corner: F is the midpoint of the step, so +6 dB at 150 Hz is only +3 dB at 150 Hz and the full +6 dB further out. Some engineers prefer a peak for focused low-end boosts, because a low shelf can sound unfocused (one engineer's experience, not a rule).

Try it: play pink noise, make LO a LO SHELF at +6 dB, then switch it to PEAK at the same F and compare. The shelf lifts all the bass; the peak lifts one bump.

Why move the frequency (F)?

Every voice, mic and room has its trouble at a different pitch, so the fix has to land on the problem. If the boxiness is at 400 Hz and the cut sits at 250 Hz, the voice only gets thinner and the box is still there.

  • Different voices: deep voices get muddy lower (about 200–300 Hz), higher voices get boxy around 400–500 Hz.
  • Different mics: headset, handheld and lapel each have their own harsh or boomy spots.
  • The room: some rooms ring or boom at one pitch.
  • Feedback: a squeal is one exact frequency, so cut it with a narrow (high Q) notch right there.
  • Goal: a little boost around 2–4 kHz adds clarity and presence; higher adds air.

The sweep trick (boost, sweep, cut)

  1. Set a band to about +6 dB with a narrow Q.
  2. Slowly turn F across the range while the person talks or sings.
  3. When the problem gets louder and uglier (boxy, harsh), you found it.
  4. Flip the gain to a cut of about −2 to −4 dB and leave F where it is.

On HEAD 1 the LO-MID band sits at 450 Hz with a small −1 dB cut: probably a slightly boxy spot on that headset mic. A different person or mic might have it somewhere else.

The high-pass filter: thud and plosives
  • It removes low rumble, handling noise and the low part of "P" pops.
  • 112 Hz is sensible for a headset mic; roughly 100–150 Hz is typical for speech, lower for deep voices.
  • Raise it slowly while someone talks and stop just before the voice sounds thin.
  • Still thudding? Try a steeper slope instead of a higher frequency, or cut the LO band a few dB with a wide Q.
  • Mic placement and a windscreen help plosives more than EQ does.
Things to try on your own
  1. Hear a band. Pink noise, EQ on. Set LO-MID to PEAK, +10 dB, and drag it left to right: low = boomy, mids = boxy or honky, highs = harsh. Cut instead of boost to hear what "mud" removal does.
  2. Notch out a ring. Switch LO-MID to NOTCH, raise Q, sweep F while the sine sweep plays. A narrow notch kills one pitch (the job of a 31-band graphic EQ, done with one band).
  3. Band pass. Switch HI-MID to BAND PASS: you hear only that slice. Handy for finding a problem frequency before you cut it.
  4. Slopes. Drag the HPF from 20 to 250 Hz on your acapella; compare 6, 12 and 18 dB at the same frequency.
  5. Delay. Use the sync test. 1 ms of delay is about 34 cm of sound travel.
Words used here

F (frequency): which pitch the band works on. G (gain): boost (+) or cut (−) in dB; 0 dB changes nothing. Q (width): higher Q is narrower. LO / LO-MID / HI-MID / HI: roughly bass, body/mud, presence, clarity/air.

Notes: the shelf shapes and Q scales are Web Audio's, close to but not identical to a real console's. The settings in head1-channel-settings.md are in the preset list as "HEAD 1".
